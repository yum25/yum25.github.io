Something I recently came across while revamping this personal site of mine was dealing with assets (most commonly, images). After working on my own site, I've noticed that within a lot of portfolio sites, assets are treated as an afterthought. This was definitely the case for me – it's easy to just take a screenshot of something as an image preview, throw it into your assets folder, and focus on the more involved aspects of coding up your website.

Recently though, I've been thinking more about bundle sizes ever since reading an article about why your website should be under 14kB in size. If you're not careful, your images can overexceed that by tens to hundreds of times, and often it doesn't have to be that way.

Sometimes while updating my site I like to look through other dev portfolios from people at a similar stage as me in their careers for inspiration. One website I came across loaded ~40 MB upon the first page load and took 15+ seconds to load the main portfolio page. Other portfolios had decent page load times, but still served images in the MB range (likely a high resolution image they got from their phone). I myself made these mistakes as well.

This figure may be a bit misleading. There are websites that ship this much data that actually run quite well! The key difference is that the 40 MB is loaded at once, meaning that rather loading it in the background, you can't see any content on the webpage until all of those megabytes are fetched.

It seems it's not just me then. This is a common problem across developers that in mild cases ships a few extra MB of data to their unsuspecting audience, and at the worst case, actively stifles their website's performance and experience. Those first few seconds of loading is important – the first 1 to 2 seconds, many have already left your page. And as the website continues buffering, more and more hop off.

This is especially a no-no on a portfolio site, where long load times could give the wrong impression of your engineering skills. All because you didn't do the trivial, easy step of trimming down your image size a little. :(

I hope I've been able to impress upon the importance of properly optimizing your images on your website! So what can we do about it?

Resize your images

Most of the times, your image does not need to be that big. Take this website you're on, for example. The image cards you see in these blogs and in my project cards don't exceed 500 pixels. Meanwhile, taking a screenshot of my laptop screen (I just did it) gives me around a 2900 x 1800 pixel image. This means if you're taking a screenshot of a project you're proud of to display, you likely already have an image that's unnecessarily big.

Your image size also depends on how large your image will be on your website itself. If you're displaying a the background image or an image the width/height of the page, you'll likely need an image size in the range of thousands of pixels. From doing a bit of experimenting, I've found that multiplying the image pixel width and height on the website by x1.7 for the actual image width and height does the trick in terms of balancing the size and the image clarity on the page.

This doesn't always work though. The images that I use on this site have hardcoded sizes - but this isn't always the case. Websites are designed to be responsive to width and height changes - it's the reason why you're able to open this same site on mobile and not have the text formatting completely break, or have to scroll left to right just to read this text through the whole width. However, this does also pose a problem for in terms of image sizing and optimization.

The srcset attribute and placeholders

This is where srcset comes in. srcset is an optional property of the HTML <img> element that allows you to define multiple image sources depending on the webpage width and height. Used with the sizes property, you can specify which images you want to associate with which range of website widths/heights.

This allows for your webpage to show the perfect image at any time - the right size for a good tradeoff between image size or load time and image quality.

Inevitably, there might be a case where despite your optimizations your assets will still take a while to load. In scenarios like this, placeholders are an elegant solution that let your audience know that there will be more for them to see if they wait a little longer.
